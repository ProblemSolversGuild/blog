What Is a Trump Account?

personal finance
Author

Hiromi Suenaga

Published

October 21, 2026

I had not looked closely at Trump Accounts until this week, when an email from the Social Security Administration landed in my inbox. It described them as investment accounts for children, said parents and guardians can claim an account in a free app, and said children born from January 1, 2025, through December 31, 2028, can receive a one-time $1,000 from the U.S. Treasury. It also said nearly 70 million accounts are already open, and that many already have money from donors waiting to be claimed. ↗ Social Security email

That was enough to make me curious. What is the account, exactly? Who receives the $1,000? And what happens to the money later? Here is what I found.

What It Is

A Trump Account is a tax-advantaged investment account for a child. It is a type of traditional individual retirement account created by federal law. The White House described it as a way for children to build long-term savings and gain an early financial stake in the U.S. economy. ↗ Enrollment · Form 4547 · Public Law 119-21 · White House

How It Works

  • $1,000 government contribution: Eligible U.S. citizen children born between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2028, with a valid Social Security number can receive a one-time $1,000 contribution from the U.S. Treasury. An authorized adult must make the required election and claim the account to receive the contribution. ↗ IRS guidance · Form 4547 · Enrollment
  • Additional contributions: Family members, employers, and other eligible contributors can add money, subject to annual contribution limits and applicable rules. Most additional contributions are subject to a combined $5,000 annual limit per child. Certain government and nonprofit contributions are treated differently, and the $1,000 pilot contribution does not count toward this limit. ↗ Form 4547
  • Low-fee investing: During childhood, investments are held in eligible index funds that track the S&P 500 or another qualifying index of primarily U.S. stocks. Annual fund fees and expenses are capped at 0.1%. ↗ Notice 2025-68
  • Access at age 18: Money generally cannot be withdrawn during childhood, subject to limited exceptions. Starting January 1 of the calendar year the child turns 18, traditional IRA tax rules generally apply. Withdrawals may be taxable and may incur an additional early-withdrawal tax. ↗ IRS guidance · Form 4547 · Early distributions
  • Financial education: The Trump Accounts app includes 15 interactive learning modules to help families learn about saving, investing, compound growth, diversification, and financial markets. ↗ Program launch

Main Pros

  • Free starting money: Eligible U.S. citizen children born between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2028, can receive a one-time $1,000 contribution from the U.S. Treasury. An authorized adult must claim the account before that money is deposited. The $1,000 is taxed when it is withdrawn. ↗ IRS guidance · Form 4547 · IRA basis · Enrollment
  • Long-term growth potential: The White House described the program as a way to give investments many years to grow through compounding. The default fund does not guarantee that result. Its value can end up higher or lower than the amount invested. ↗ White House · Default fund
  • Tax advantages: The account is tax-deferred, so growth is not taxed while the money stays in the account. Money a family adds does not qualify for a tax deduction. The amount the family contributed can come out later without being taxed again, and the growth on it is taxed when withdrawn. After childhood, normal IRA withdrawal rules apply. ↗ White House · Form 4547 · IRA basis
  • Low investment fund expenses: Eligible investment funds must keep annual fund fees and expenses to no more than 0.1% of the fund. That cap is the fund’s own fees and expenses. A charge the fund does not impose, such as a broker’s sales commission, is outside the cap. ↗ Notice 2025-68 · Fund Choices
  • Learning opportunities: The app includes 15 interactive financial education modules covering saving, investing, compound growth, diversification, and the role of capital markets. ↗ Program launch
  • Multiple funding sources: Depending on the applicable rules, contributions may come from individuals, employers, and certain governments or nonprofit organizations. ↗ IRS guidance

Main Cons

  • Limited access during childhood: Distributions are generally not allowed during the growth period, which ends on December 31 of the calendar year the child turns 17. Limited exceptions apply. Traditional IRA rules generally apply beginning in the calendar year the child turns 18. ↗ Form 4547
  • Investment risk: The account invests in stock-index funds. The default fund states that the investment is not guaranteed and that its value can be higher or lower than the amount invested. ↗ Fund Choices · Default fund
  • Limited investment choices during childhood: Investments must be in eligible mutual funds or ETFs tracking the S&P 500 or another qualifying index of primarily U.S. equities, with no leverage and fund expenses within the statutory cap. ↗ Notice 2025-68 · Fund Choices
  • Taxes and potential penalties after age 18: The $1,000, employer contributions, and certain government or nonprofit contributions are taxed when withdrawn. Money a family adds is not taxed a second time, but the growth on that money is. A taxable early withdrawal may be subject to ordinary income tax and an additional 10% tax before age 59½, unless an exception applies. Qualifying higher-education expenses and a qualifying first-home purchase can avoid the additional 10% tax under traditional IRA rules, but that does not automatically make the withdrawal income-tax-free. ↗ Form 4547 · IRA basis · Early distributions
  • Contribution limits: Most contributions from individuals, including family and friends, count toward a combined annual $5,000 limit per child during the growth period. The $1,000 pilot contribution and certain qualified general contributions from governments and nonprofits do not count toward that limit; certain employer contributions do count. ↗ Form 4547

Why Were Trump Accounts Created?

The White House presented the program as a way to give children an early financial stake in the U.S. economy. The idea is to invest money early, allow it to grow over many years, and help children learn about saving, investing, and compound growth. ↗ White House · Program launch

The administration introduced the proposal in June 2025 as part of the One Big Beautiful Bill. Business leaders and lawmakers participating in the announcement discussed the potential benefits of early investing and giving children a financial head start. ↗ White House

History and Timeline

June 9, 2025 — Announced

President Trump publicly introduced Trump Accounts at a White House event with business leaders and lawmakers. ↗ White House

July 4, 2025 — Became law

President Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill into law, establishing Trump Accounts through federal legislation. ↗ Public Law 119-21

December 2, 2025 — Initial guidance

The IRS and Treasury issued initial guidance on eligibility, contributions, investments, and other program rules. ↗ IRS guidance

May 28, 2026 — App launched

Treasury announced the Trump Accounts app and the start of account activation for families. ↗ App launch

July 4, 2026 — Full program launch

Treasury announced that the full app was live, families could manage accounts and contribute, and the app included 15 interactive financial education modules. ↗ Program launch

October 1, 2026 — Automatic enrollment completed

Treasury announced that eligible children under 18 with valid Social Security numbers had an account ready to be claimed. A parent or guardian must claim the account to manage it. The $1,000 is separate: it goes only to U.S. citizens born between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2028, and only after the account is claimed. ↗ Enrollment

Bottom Line

Trump Accounts are designed to give eligible children a head start on long-term investing through a one-time government contribution, additional contributions under the program rules, and tax-deferred investment growth. Families should also understand that withdrawals are generally restricted during childhood, stock-market investments can lose value, and traditional IRA tax rules generally apply after the growth period. The $1,000 is taxed when it is withdrawn, and money a family adds is not tax-deductible. ↗ IRS guidance · White House · Form 4547 · IRA basis · Default fund

Just for Illustration: What Could $1,000 Grow Into?

Now that we’ve covered the official program information, let’s imagine one simple scenario: a child receives the $1,000 starting contribution, nobody adds any more money, and the money stays invested for the long term.

The table below is only an illustration. It is not a government projection, a promise, or a prediction of what a Trump Account will earn. It simply shows how different hypothetical rates of growth can lead to different outcomes over time.

What this means: With a hypothetical 7% average annual return, the original $1,000 could grow to about $3,400 by age 18 or $56,000 by age 59½, even if nobody adds another dollar. The amount could be lower or higher in real life.

A few simple notes:

  • For an easy comparison, this example assumes the $1,000 is invested from birth and stays invested, with no additional contributions or withdrawals. If investment starts later, there is less time for growth.
  • The percentages are examples, not forecasts. Real investment returns go up and down, and the account can lose value.
  • These amounts are not adjusted for inflation and do not show taxes that might be due when money is withdrawn.
  • Age 59½ is included as a familiar retirement-account milestone; the numbers show only a hypothetical account balance, not whether or how a withdrawal would be taxed.
